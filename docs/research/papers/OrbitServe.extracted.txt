Space or Not? Constellation-Scale Simulation and
State-Aware Scheduling for Orbital LLM Serving
Anonymous Author(s)
Submission Id: 1184
Abstract
Rapidly growing demand for large language model (LLM)
inference is increasing the power and infrastructure required
to scale serving capacity. Orbital computing offers a new de-
ployment environment in low Earth orbit (LEO), where on-
board solar generation can reduce dependence on terrestrial
power and datacenter infrastructure. However, provisioning
compute and power in orbit does not guarantee usable LLM
serving capacity for terrestrial users. Orbital motion contin-
uously changes ground access availability and inter-satellite
communication paths, while request execution consumes
onboard energy and affects serving states. Quantifying this
gap and identifying the underlying bottlenecks require these
coupled dynamics to be modeled jointly.
In this paper, we introduce OrbitServe, a constellation-
scale simulation framework for evaluating orbital LLM serv-
ing. By integrating a time-resolved constellation model with
a state-coupled runtime simulation engine, OrbitServe re-
plays geo-temporal workloads and simulates end-to-end re-
quest execution on a shared event timeline. Using OrbitServe,
we characterize the operational constraints on orbital LLM
serving. Communication relays mitigate access delays un-
der insufficient ground coverage, while sustained serving is
constrained by time-varying solar availability and battery ca-
pacity. These findings motivate a state-aware scheduler that
jointly accounts for the time-varying coupled states. Exten-
sive experiments show that the proposed scheduler reduces
P95 time-to-first-token (TTFT) by up to 95.3% compared to
several runtime scheduling policies. By leveraging Orbit-
Serve, we further highlight future research directions for
orbital LLM serving and show that with an idealized dense
communication constellation, orbital LLM serving perfor-
mance can be comparable to that of the terrestrial datacenter
with the same compute capacity.

Building on this industry momentum, we envision an or-
bital LLM serving system deployed in low Earth orbit (LEO).
A compute constellation is organized into compute cells,
each comprising one or more accelerator-equipped satel-
lites. Requests from terrestrial users are forwarded through
ground gateways and relayed by communication satellites
to one compute cell. The compute cell then executes LLM
inference and returns the reponse to the user. Three develop-
ments support the feasibility of such an orbital LLM serving
system. Firstly, sunlight exposure in LEO can be 4–5× longer
than that on the ground, together with 36% higher photo-
voltaic condition [5, 33]. Secondly, reusable launch vehicles
and standardized rideshare services can reduce the launch
cost. With first-stage recovery and reuse, analyses estimate
recurring launch costs of below $1,000 per kilogram [ 15].
Thirdly, for the radiation-induced faults, a carefully selected
commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) platform was validated in a
32-hour heavy-ion irradiation experiment [10].
Prior work has established technical foundations for LLM
inference in space. However, an operational LLM serving
system must coordinate the communication and compute re-
sources to meet latency and service-continuity requirements.
Different from terrestrial datacenters, in space, the dynamic
orbital and runtime states continuously change the availabil-
ity and utilization of these resources. Figure. 1 demonstrates
the key characteristics of the orbital LLM serving system:
Time-varying ground access.Terrestrial datacenters
are geographically fixed and typically provide continuous
network access to their compute resources. In contrast, a
satellite continuously moves relative to the ground and is

directly reachable from a gateway only during the visibility
window. As shown in Figure. 1(a), a satellite in a 550-km
orbit has a period of approximately 95.5 minutes, yet remains
visible to a ground gateway for only about 4.5 minutes during
an overhead pass. Therefore, when no feasible gateway-to-
satellite path is available, requests must wait for connectivity
even if compute resources are idle.
Time-varying inter-satellite communication.In ter-
restrial datacenters, compute nodes remain at fixed locations
and communicate over a relatively stable physical network
topology. In orbit, data transfers across compute cells rely
on inter-satellite links (ISLs). As shown in Figure. 1(b), satel-
lite motion continuously changes the feasible ISL topology
and distances between satellites. An existing ISL becomes
unavailable when the distance between two connected satel-
lites exceeds the link’s maximum operating range. Therefore,
transfers at different times may traverse varying ISL paths
and incur different delays.
Coupled orbital and runtime states.Orbital states, in-
cluding satellite position and onboard energy availability,
evolve with orbital motion and constrain the feasible exe-
cution plans for each request. As shown in Figure. 1(c), the
satellite during eclipse may be unable to execute requests
with insufficient battery energy. Request forwarding and
inference, in turn, occupy link and compute resources while
consuming onboard energy. Therefore, request scheduling
must consider how current execution affects resource avail-
ability encountered by subsequent requests.
Given the above differences, two fundamental questions
arise: (1)What factors limit the conversion of nominal orbital
resources into achievable LLM serving performance?(2)How
should requests be scheduled across moving compute cells to
mitigate these limitations in orbital LLM serving?
Existing simulation tools support orbital analysis [3], net-
work simulation [23, 26] and onboard-system modeling [14].
However, these tools are not designed for orbit LLM serv-
ing and lack support for modeling how coupled orbital and
runtime states affect serving performance. To address this
modeling gap, we introduce OrbitServe, a constellation-scale
simulation framework for evaluating orbital LLM serving,
which mainly incorporates two components. Firstly, the
time-resolved constellation modelcombines parameter-
ized satellite resources with orbital and communication con-
figurations to support the simulation of flexible constellation
designs. The model derives the time-varying orbital and com-
munication states, together with an analytical serving model
for the compute cells. Secondly, thestate-coupled runtime
simulation enginereplays geo-temporal workloads, group-
ing one or more turns from the same conversation into a
session, and simulates end-to-end request execution on a
shared event timeline. OrbitServe further captures the cou-
pling effect between orbital and runtime dynamics through
event-driven state updates. The main contributions of this
work are as follows:
• We introduce OrbitServe, a constellation-scale simulation
framework for orbital LLM serving. It integrates a time-
resolved constellation model with a state-coupled runtime
engine to capture the interaction between orbital dynam-
ics and request execution. To the best of our knowledge,
OrbitServe is the first framework that enables end-to-end
performance evaluation of orbital LLM serving under geo-
temporal workloads.
• Using OrbitServe, we quantitatively characterize the key
constraints of orbital LLM serving. (1) Compute constella-
tions without sufficient coverage require communication
relays for timely ground access, (2) sustained serving de-
pends on energy provisioning and runtime power manage-
ment, and (3) the nearest compute cell may be suboptimal
once compute load and key-value (KV) cache locality are
considered. These findings motivate runtime scheduling
design that accounts for the time-varying coupled states.
• We propose a state-aware runtime scheduler, aiming to
minimize average end-to-end (E2E) latency while satisfy-
ing the service-level objective (SLO) target. The scheduler
transforms evolving states into forwarding efficiency, com-
pute load, energy availability, and intra-session continuity
metrics. These metrics are combined together to achieve
scenario-specific scheduling priorities.
Extensive experiments across different workloads and con-
figurations show that, compared to several runtime sched-
uling policies, the proposed state-aware scheduler reduces
P95 time-to-first-token (TTFT) by up to 95.3% and achieves
1.40×-3.19× higher SLO goodput in an energy-constrained
scenario. By leveraging OrbitServe, we further identify fu-
ture research directions for orbital LLM serving and show
that with an idealized dense communication constellation,
orbital LLM serving achieves E2E latency comparable to that
of an equal-capacity terrestrial datacenter.
2 Background and Motivation
2.1 LLM Serving
LLM inference consists of two stages:Prefillprocesses the
input prompt, whiledecodestage generates output tokens
one at a time. The key-value (KV) cache stores attention key
and value tensors for reuse during generation. A session
is a conversation containing one or more turns. Requests
with a shared prompt prefix can reuse cached KV states to
reduce prefill computation, both within a multi-turn session
and across sessions. LLM requests come from geographically
distributed users, with volumes varying across regions and
throughout the day [4, 69]. Requests also differ in prompt
length, output length, and reusable context, so equal request
counts may exhibit different computational demands.
Serving performance is evaluated by response latency and
the rate of completed requests meeting the SLO. Time-to-
first-token (TTFT) measures the delay from request submis-
sion to receipt of the first output token, while end-to-end

(E2E) latency measures the delay to receipt of the complete
response. For a response containing𝑁> 1output tokens, the
average time-per-output-token (TPOT) is(𝑇E2E−𝑇TTFT)/(𝑁−
1) [34]. An SLO specifies performance requirements, such as
limits on TTFT and TPOT [68]. SLO goodput is defined as
the number of completed requests per second meeting these
limits [56]. To reduce response latency and improve serv-
ing throughput, distributed LLM serving systems consider
both replica load and prefix reuse when assigning requests
to model replicas [50, 60].
2.2 Orbital Computing Infrastructure
Orbital computing uses processors aboard satellites to exe-
cute computing workloads. A compute satellite consists of
a computing payload and a spacecraft bus. The payload in-
cludes processors and storage, while the bus provides power,
thermal control, communications, and orbit control [18, 31].
Starcloud-1 carried an NVIDIA H100 GPU into space in
November 2025 [51]. Such hardware introduces substantial
power and thermal loads. For reference, the H100 SXM has a
maximum thermal design power (TDP) of 700 W [35], while
an NVL72 rack is rated at 140 kW, illustrating the wide range
of power requirements across hardware configurations [53].
Supporting these loads requires power generation, energy
storage, heat transport, and radiators [8, 55].
Solar arrays supply operating power and recharge batter-
ies during sunlight, while batteries support operation during
eclipse. Orbital configuration affects the sunlight exposure
and eclipse duration, and therefore influences power-system
sizing [17, 29]. The surrounding vacuum precludes convec-
tive heat transfer to the external environment, so spacecraft
must transport heat to radiating surfaces and reject it into
space [19, 30]. S𝜇DC estimates that approximately 4 m2 of
radiator panel area can reject 4 kW of heat at 45◦C, assum-
ing an emissivity of 0.86 and both panel faces viewing deep
space [8]. Increasing the computing load requires larger solar
arrays, batteries, and radiators, increasing spacecraft mass
and required launch capacity [8, 55].
Ground-satellite links (GSLs) connect ground gateways to
satellites, commonly using radio-frequency communication.
Inter-satellite links (ISLs) relay data between satellites using
lasers [21, 32]. Starlink reports ISL bandwidth up to 200 Gbps,
while its V3 design specifies 400 Gbps laser terminals [47, 49].
The achievable bandwidth of a laser link depends on trans-
mit power, aperture size, pointing accuracy, and link dis-
tance. With otherwise identical configurations, longer links
increase propagation delay and decrease received optical
power, limiting the achievable bandwidth [16, 61].
2.3 Motivation
The resources described in Section 2.2 support orbital com-
puting, but their specifications alone do not determine serv-
ing performance. As illustrated in Figure 1, satellite motion
limits direct access from a fixed ground gateway. For a rep-
resentative 550-km circular orbit with a minimum elevation
angle of25 ◦ [23], an ideal overhead pass spans only about
4.7% of an orbital period. Constellation layout and relay con-
nectivity determine whether access is available outside this
window. Energy availability also varies: eclipse can occupy
up to approximately 36% of an orbital period at this altitude,
while a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit design can pro-
vide near-continuous sunlight [1]. These differences raise a
common question: under what deployment conditions can
orbital compute provide timely responses to ground users?
Answering this question also requires considering how
serving conditions change during request execution. Trans-
fer delays affect when a request reaches a compute cell and
the queue length and inference concurrency it encounters.
Its execution then occupies compute resources, consumes
energy, and updates KV-cache state for later turns. When
the next turn arrives, satellite motion may have changed the
path to the cell holding the session’s KV-cache, while other
requests have changed that cell’s load and battery charge.
Local KV-reuse, cache migration, and context recomputation
can incur different costs. A simulator must therefore track
each request from arrival to completion and account for
changes in connectivity, compute load, energy availability,
and KV-cache state during inference.
3 OrbitServe Framework
We propose OrbitServe, a constellation-scale simulation frame-
work to evaluate orbital LLM serving under coupled orbital,
communication, energy, and serving dynamics. In this sec-
tion, we introduce the design of OrbitServe (Section 3.1),
including its two key components: theTime-Resolved Con-
stellation Model(Section 3.2) and theState-Coupled Runtime
Simulation Engine(Section 3.3).
3.1 OrbitServe Overview
To quantify how orbital compute capacity transforms into
LLM serving performance, OrbitServe takes the constellation
configuration, geo-temporal workload trace, and runtime
scheduling policy as inputs. It jointly simulates constellation
and runtime dynamics to capture their coupled effects on
resource availability and request execution. Finally, it outputs
per-request execution records and aggregate performance
metrics at the session, satellite and request levels.

3.2 Time-Resolved Constellation Model
Hierarchical constellation representations.OrbitServe
represents a compute constellation using a four-level hier-
archy. At theSatellitelevel, each satellite is instantiated via
the parameterized resources. At theCompute Celllevel, one
or more tightly coordinated compute satellites jointly host
LLM replicas. Each compute cell is configured by its satellite
composition, intra-cell topology and inter-satellite commu-
nication bandwidth. OrbitServe models intra-cell topology
as a connectivity graph with distance and bandwidth at-
tributes on each edge. The connection patterns and link
bandwidths are configurable for different cell designs. At the
Orbital Planelevel, multiple compute cells are distributed
along the same orbital plane. Each plane specifies its orbital
geometry, including altitude and inclination. For inter-cell
communication, OrbitServe treats each compute cell as a sin-
gle logical endpoint. At theConstellationlevel, one or more
orbital planes form a full compute constellation.

3.3 State-Coupled Runtime Simulation Engine
OrbitServe replays workload traces and simulates request
execution on a shared event timeline. Each event represents
a timestamped occurrence, such as request arrival or dis-
patch, the start or completion of a communication flow, or
the completion of inference. Processing these events updates
the coupled orbital and runtime states, which influence sub-
sequent request execution in turn.
Geo-temporal workload replay.OrbitServe loads stan-
dardized workload bundles, each containing request and ses-
sion identifiers, turn indices, ingress gateways, arrival times,
and input/output lengths. The replayer generates a request
arrival event using its specified time and ground gateway on
the shared event timeline, initiating the state-coupled sim-
ulation workflow described below. This preserves regional
differences in request volume and diurnal request patterns
encoded in the bundles, as illustrated in Figure 4. The work-
load replayer also preserves session associations and turn
ordering, which identify the context available for KV-cache
reuse across turns within the same session. Workload in-
tensity is configurable. Fixing the replay configuration and
random seed yields a reproducible request stream.
State-coupled simulation workflow.OrbitServe ad-
vances request execution on a shared event timeline, cou-
pling communication, compute, session, and energy states.
At each event, the engine evaluates data dependencies and
resource availability, then coordinates the corresponding re-
sources to advance the simulation. The state updates are read
by subsequent events, allowing earlier scheduling decisions
and request execution to influence later requests. Figure 5
illustrates how communication, queueing, and inference ex-
ecution interact with request and KV-cache state updates.
Communication events determine when the required data

4 Characterizing Orbital LLM Serving
Delivering a responsive and sustainable orbital LLM ser-
vice requires coordination across three dimensions: timely
ground access to orbital compute, sufficient energy to sus-
tain the serving across orbital cycles, and effective request
scheduling among dynamic compute cells. A bottleneck in
any dimension can prevent nominal compute capacity from
meeting serving SLOs. Using OrbitServe, we quantitatively
characterize these constraints in this section.
4.1 Ground Access Availability
Timely ground access determines whether provisioned or-
bital compute capacity can serve interactive requests. We
focus on direct ground access to compute satellites (without
communication satelites) and examine how constellation
scale affects access waiting time. When no compute satellite
is visible to a request’s ground gateway upon arrival, the
request must wait for one to become visible.
We replay a 24-hour workload across 35 ground gate-
ways and use OrbitServe’s orbital model to evaluate 550-km
Walker-like constellations containing 8 to 1,024 compute
satellites. A compute satellite is directly accessible when its
elevation angle at the corresponding gateway exceeds 25°.
As shown in Figure. 6, direct access cannot reliably support
interactive LLM serving when the compute constellation
contains only 8-128 satellites. Some requests can wait tens of
minutes or even several hours before any compute satellite
becomes visible. With 256 satellites, 94.4% of requests incur
no access waiting, but the P99 access wait remains nearly
100 s. Continuous direct access is achieved only with 512

4.2 Energy Sustainability
Solar generation varies with orbital illumination, whereas
inference demand and standby power persist during eclipse.
Therefore, solar-array power determines the energy har-
vested during sunlit periods, while battery capacity buffers
the temporary energy deficit during eclipse. To quantify
the single-orbit battery capacity requirement, we use Orbit-
Serve to simulate a satellite equipped with eight NVIDIA
B300 GPUs serving Kimi-K3 under a saturated workload.
The workload comprises back-to-back of batches of 64 re-
quests, each with 2,048 input tokens and 256 output tokens,
with no idle interval between batches. The representative
Walker orbit has a period of approximately 95.5 minutes, of
which 36.0% is spent in eclipse. Figure. 7 reports the mini-
mum required battery capacity that avoids battery depletion
over one orbit. A battery smaller than 2 kWh cannot sustain
computation through the 34.4-minute eclipse.
Dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS) decreases
accelerator voltage and operating frequency to trade serving
performance for energy efficiency. At a solar-array power of
5 kW, setting the DVFS scale to 0.5 during eclipse reduces
the minimum required battery capacity from 2 to 1 kWh,
but decreases the estimated number of requests completed
per orbit by 18.0%. This tradeoff motivates energy-aware
scheduling and runtime power control under constrained
onboard energy budgets.

5.3 Runtime Scheduling Procedure
For each arriving request, the scheduler reads the current
states and performs the following steps:
(1) Candidate filtering.A compute cell remains eligible
only if its predicted minimum SOC during request execution
exceeds the configured threshold. Cells at the concurrency
limit remain eligible because requests can be queued. Failed
cells are excluded. If the selected cell fails after scheduling,
the request is reassigned to another eligible cell.
(2) Metric evaluation.For each eligible cell, the scheduler
computes the four metrics defined in Section 5.2.
(3) Weighted aggregation.The scheduler aggregates
the metrics using configurable weights. For request 𝑟𝑖 and

When migration is enabled, missing reusable KV-cache is
transferred only if the predicted migration cost is lower than
that of context recomputation; otherwise, the missing con-

cache transfers complete, the request enters the cell’s queue
and starts inference when a slot becomes available.
6 Evaluation
Using OrbitServe, we evaluate the performance of orbital
LLM serving across different real-world workloads and con-
stellation configurations to investigate the key questions:
• What factors limit the conversion of nominal orbital re-
sources into delivered LLM serving capacity?
• How should requests be scheduled across moving com-
pute cells to mitigate these limitations?
6.1 Experimental Setup
The evaluation is focused on the LEO constellations at an
altitude of 550 km. We evaluate two compute-cell configura-
tions. The B300 configuration consists of a single compute
satellite carrying eight NVIDIA B300 GPUs, whereas the
H200 configuration consists of two compute satellites, each
equipped with 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs [ 36, 37]. Each com-
pute cell hosts one Kimi-K3 instance [24] and supports up
to 64 concurrent requests. The B300 configuration uses TP8,
whereas the H200 configuration uses PP2×TP8. We set the
GSL bandwidth to 1 Gbps. The bandwidths of inter- and
intra-cell ISLs are set to 400 Gbps and 1 Tbps, respectively.
Unless otherwise specified, all experiments adopt the same
model and per-satellite hardware configuration. We evaluate
both communication settings described in Section. 3.2.
Workloads.We construct two 24-hour workload traces
using dataset-specific geographic and temporal mappings.
For ShareChat, each session is assigned to one of 123 coun-
tries using Anthropic’s country shares, with separate esti-
mates for China and Russia [4, 28, 59, 63]. Ten geographically
large or high-demand countries are further divided into sub-
regions, yielding 145 regions in total. Each request is routed
to the nearest of 35 ground gateways, with its arrival time
sampled from the corresponding local-time request distribu-
tion [69]. For WildChat, we retain the country- or state-level
location for each request, and map the conversations in May
2023 into the corresponding time-of-day bins to construct a
representative 24-hour trace.

Comparison Baselines.To explore orbital runtime sched-
uling strategies, we compare the state-aware balanced sched-
uler with several intuitive solutions:
• Latency-only:Computes the request routing latency and
selects the feasible compute cell with the lowest estimated
request latency.
• Load-only:Tracks the number of running requests per
cell and routes each new request to the cell with the most
available compute capacity, similar to the terrestrial Least
Load policy [54].
• KV-only:Prioritizes the cell holding the largest amount
of reusable KV-cache state for the requesting session.
• Round Robin:Distributes incoming requests cyclically
across feasible compute cells without considering their
current runtime states [54].
Metrics.We evaluate the TTFT latency and SLO goodput
as the primary efficiency metrics. The SLO goodput is defined
as the number of completed requests per second satisfying
both TTFT≤2 s and TPOT≤200 ms/token.

6.5 Space or Not Characterization
Using the same serving model, we compare orbital and ter-
restrial deployments with 128 H200-based Kimi-K3 replicas
each. Compute-satellite and communication-satellite relays
use the hop-by-hop and arc-based models, respectively; the
terrestrial setup follows Section 6.2. We first examine the
effect of limited ground access. For compute-satellite relay,
P95 access waiting time is approximately 1,500 s and P95
E2E latency is approximately 1,510 s. Relying on the com-
pute constellation for ground access causes extremely long
response latency. With communication-satellite relay, P95
E2E latency remains near 5.36 s across workloads, close to
Ground’s 5.50–5.55 s. Both deployments also exhibit similar
TPOT, with medians near 1.96 ms/token and P95 values of
2.006–2.062 ms/token. Under the continuous connectivity
and bandwidth assumptions of the dense-relay model, or-
bital serving can achieve latencies comparable to those of an
equal-capacity terrestrial deployment.
7 Related Work
Space-computing infrastructure and systems.Google
proposes solar-powered TPU satellite clusters with optical
interconnects [1]. Other studies examine datacenter econom-
ics [55], lifecycle emissions [22, 38], and battery aging [65].
Prior work processes space-generated data under compute,
communication, and energy constraints [8, 9, 13, 14, 20, 27,
44, 46, 66]. Other studies address task and application sched-
uling in satellite networks [7, 25, 41–43, 64].
Orbital LLM systems.SpaceMoE [ 57] studies expert place-
ment for a single MoE replica across multiple satellites and
evaluates request-level token-generation latency, doesn’t re-
port serving-level request-trace or cross-replica scheduling.
SkyMemory [45] compares distributed KV-cache layouts for
a fixed model host.
Terrestrial LLM serving.Prior systems optimize resource
allocation [39, 68], request migration [52], and prefix reuse
with load balancing [50]. SkyWalker [60] uses locality-aware
cross-region routing to balance diurnal demand. In orbit,
scheduling must also account for time-varying ground access
and inter-satellite communication paths.
LEO simulation frameworks.Existing tools support or-
bital analysis [3], network simulation [23, 26], software em-
ulation [40], and onboard-system modeling [14]. Differently,
OritServe evaluates geo-temporal LLM serving performance
and SLO goodput under geo-temporal workloads while ac-
counting for orbital dynamics.
8 Conclusion
We present OrbitServe, a constellation-scale simulation frame-
work for evaluating orbital LLM serving performance under
coupled orbital and runtime dynamics. The characterization
using OrbitServe motivates a state-aware runtime scheduler
that jointly accounts for the time-varying coupled states.
Our evaluation shows that the proposed compared to several
scheduling policies, our scheduler reduces P95 TTFT by up
to 95.3% and achieves 1.40×–3.19× higher SLO goodput in
an energy-constrained scenario. Under idealized communi-
cation relaying, orbital serving achieves a P95 E2E latency
of 5.36 s, comparable to 5.55 s for a terrestrial baseline with
matched compute capacity. The results highlight the impor-
tance of communication provisioning and runtime schedul-
ing for orbital LLM serving.
